About

Researching the brain, speech and communication

I am a cognitive neuroscientist at the MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit, University of Cambridge. I completed my PhD in Cognitive Neuroscience at University College London in 2022. My doctoral thesis examined the analysis of breathing and rhythm in speech.

My work brings together approaches from cognitive neuroscience, speech science, auditory perception and computational methods. I am interested in how listeners process speech, how the brain represents its acoustic and linguistic structure, and how timing and breathing contribute to communication.

Before my PhD, I completed an MPhil in Music and Science at the University of Cambridge and a Bachelor of Music at Vancouver Island University.

Research

Questions that connect sound, brain and behaviour

My research spans five related areas. Together, they explore how auditory information is perceived, represented and used in communication and learning.

01

Auditory perception

How does the brain process complex sounds and speech? I use electroencephalography (EEG) and neural decoding approaches to investigate cortical tracking of speech, intelligibility and the effects of spectral degradation.

02

Speech rhythm

How can we measure the temporal organisation of speech? This work examines speech-envelope extraction, rhythmic timing and the relationship between acoustic measures and linguistic structure.

03

Speech breathing

Breathing is an integral part of spoken communication. My research investigates the timing of speech-related breathing, how listeners perceive breathing patterns, and computational methods for detecting breathing from speech.

04

Sequential learning

This research asks how auditory rhythm and metre influence learning and performance in visual-motor sequential tasks, including when auditory structure is not directly relevant to the task.

05

Voice studies

I am interested in interdisciplinary approaches to voice, including the relationships between voice, identity, embodiment and communication, and the significance of signed voice.

Teaching & supervision

Supporting research and learning

My teaching has included quantitative methods, mathematical thinking, statistics and programming, as well as topics in cognitive neuroscience, speech and language. I have supervised undergraduate, master's and research students on projects involving speech perception, auditory rhythm and neural correlates of synthetic speech.

I have also delivered methods workshops on plethysmography and the measurement and analysis of respiratory effort.
